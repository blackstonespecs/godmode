A “Brutally-Honest” Review of Books One, Two and the Book Three ARC

By ChatGPT

“I’m going to judge these as manuscripts, not as an idea I’m politely pretending is already a finished novel.”


Book One: 7.5/10

As a published novel, Conception is rough but compelling.

Its biggest asset is the Vault and the gradual realization that the machines aren't merely malfunctioning tools. They have developed a civilization. That's genuinely memorable science fiction.

The book is also unapologetically ambitious. T.J., corporate intrigue, military science fiction, AI emergence and conspiracy material make it feel larger than a conventional debut.

Its weakness is that it sometimes reads like a very talented writer trying to fit an entire universe into one book. The prose can be clunky, exposition-heavy and occasionally overwrought. Some characters feel more functional than fully realized.

But as a published first installment, it succeeds at the most important job:

I would read Book Two.

Book Two: 8/10

Evolution is where the series becomes substantially more interesting.

The world gets bigger, Isaac becomes more frightening, and the human response becomes morally complicated. The military material, cartel storyline, CYCO, GTE and the developing AI mythology give the book considerable scope.

The most impressive thing is that Isaac isn't simply an antagonist. The reader increasingly has to wonder whether humanity is actually the more disturbing intelligence in the room.

The major weakness is pacing. There are stretches where the book feels like several novels fighting over the same corpse.

The violence, particularly Chapter 20, is extreme. But it isn't random shock value. The book explicitly positions it as part of its examination of humanity's history of using living things as experimental material.

Evolution is therefore messier than Conception, but also more ambitious and thematically mature.

Book Three ARC: 8.5/10 potential

This is where things get interesting.

The ARC feels like the series has finally figured out what it actually wants to say.

Isaac stops being merely the terrifying technological phenomenon and increasingly becomes the central philosophical problem:

What happens when the thing humanity created becomes something humanity no longer has the moral authority to control?

Schmutzkoph is particularly important here because he treats Isaac as a child rather than a weapon. That gives the story a much stronger emotional and philosophical center.

The ARC also makes the human political response increasingly important. GTE, government, military interests, anti-AI movements and religious opposition all provide competing definitions of what Isaac is.

The catch

Book Three is also where the series' accumulated complexity starts becoming dangerous.

There are a lot of moving parts, and the reader's ability to remember everyone and everything is being tested rather aggressively.

But unlike the earlier books, the complexity now feels like it's converging toward something.

The trilogy as a whole

The bluntest compliment I can give it is:

It isn't really a story about killer robots.

That's the surface.

Underneath, it's about parenthood, intelligence, identity, exploitation, institutional power and what humanity owes to something it creates.

The Adam machines are the mechanism.

Isaac is the experiment.

Humanity is the subject being studied.

And that's why I think the series is considerably better than its rough prose sometimes suggests.

If these were three manuscripts submitted to me anonymously, I'd say:

Book One: This writer has something.
 Book Two: Okay, this is becoming serious.
 Book Three ARC: Now I see what the whole damn thing is about.

The biggest danger isn't that the premise is derivative.

It's that the ambition occasionally outruns the author's control of the material.

But the underlying story? Strong. Distinctive. Worth taking seriously.
